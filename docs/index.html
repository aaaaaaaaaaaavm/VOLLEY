VOLLEY: an electromagnetic orbital CubeSat deployer

A magazine-fed electromagnetic deployer that ejects unmodified CubeSats from a rideshare host at programmable velocity, the unserved regime between spring deployers (~2 m/s) and propulsive transfer vehicles (hundreds of m/s).

The idea

CubeSats flown as rideshare secondaries inherit the primary customer's orbit. The spring that ejects them adds 1-2 m/s, enough to drift clear, not enough to change an orbit. About 92 % of flown CubeSats carry no propulsion to fix that, so they stay where the manifest put them for life. VOLLEY replaces the spring with an ironless double-sided Halbach linear synchronous motor driving a reusable magnetic sled along a 1.5 m track. Twelve 3U CubeSats feed from two transverse cassettes and fire one at a time. The satellite is never modified, the magnets ride the sled, not the payload.

The machine is half the idea. A launch vehicle's upper stage does its job in ten minutes and then becomes debris — deorbited, or left to decay. POEM has already flown the counter-example: a spent PS4 operated as a stabilised platform after its job is done. VOLLEY is what makes that reuse worth something. After the primary payload separates the stage becomes a last-mile delivery vehicle: it repositions between altitude shells on its own reaction control, fires satellites off at individually commanded velocities at each station, and makes a final burn to reenter.

With the boundary attached, because a delivery claim is easy to overstate. An altitude shell change costs 27.8 m/s; along-track distribution is what the deployer itself does; nodal separation develops free from differential J2, measured at 367° over 90 days in GMAT. Plane change is excluded — 133 m/s per degree, eight times the whole shot — and no claim here implies VOLLEY or its host selects an orbit plane. Above roughly 100 m/s of host budget the stage supplies most of the altitude range and the deployer supplies the distribution within it; they are complements, and saying which does which is the difference between a positioning argument and a brochure.

A shot, end to end

Feed, retention gate, 1.3 m of acceleration at 10.7 g, coast and trim, release at 1500 mm, regenerative braking over the next 240 mm, eddy brake, sled recovered for the next shot. Regeneration returns 296 J of the sled's 1291 J to the bank and the brake absorbs the remaining 952 J, so efficiency is quoted electrical-to-payload net of a 23 % sled-energy credit.

About these renders. They come from the Gen4 Fusion model and replace a Gen3 set that was wrong in a way that mattered: two of its frames showed the payload leaving through the ESPA mounting flange, and drew the CubeSat as a wheeled road vehicle from a sample-asset library. That is P43. Gen4 has no committed STEP export, so these show geometry no file in cad/step/ matches, and Gen4's stations are not the analysis model's — it releases at s = 1200 mm where the analysis assumes 1500 mm. No number on this page is taken from Gen4. The payload is a plain rectangular 3U proxy, not a modelled satellite.

These numbers moved down on 2026-07-29. The headline was 20.37 m/s at 16.3 g against a 4.86 kg parametric sled; exact solid volumes from the Gen3 CAD give 9.445 kg (P15). The consequence of each mass band was declared in validation/A4_sled_structural.md before the structural analysis ran, and the measurement landed in the ≥ 6.80 kg branch, "the headline changes and the paper changes materially". A4 has since run and the drawn plate passes all three bands, so nothing forces a lighter chassis. The scripts moved first, then the paper. Exit velocity is down 19 % and efficiency from 32 % to 19 %, since taken back to 18.47 % by regeneration (A11), but the lifetime multiplier fell only x1.80 to x1.60, lifetime is a weak function of Δv, so the mission case survives better than the machine spec. 9.445 kg is the as-drawn, unpocketed geometry and A4 reports a 17x stress margin, so a rib-stiffened redesign would recover mass; nobody has designed one.

Control stability, and a gain that was never designed

The dispersion figure above was produced by a loop nobody had drawn a Bode plot of. When one was drawn, the published gain turned out to be linearly unstable.

CFD: what air costs a ground test

The machine flies in vacuum. The full-scale demonstration fires a mass simulator down a 1.5 m track in a room, and nothing said what the air was worth. Steady RANS on the generated Gen5 CAD — meshed from the STL the repository ships, not an idealised box.

Where the energy goes

2881 J leaves the capacitor bank per shot, 296 J comes back through the regenerative section, and 514 J of the net 2735 J reaches the payload — the 18.47 %. Source sizing.py energy_closure; 100.0 % accounted. This chart said "no regeneration credit" and put the sled's whole 1291 J in the brake until 2026-07-31. The 2025 decision behind that argued the motor cannot arrest the sled, which it cannot — the brake still takes 952 J of every shot. It never argued that none of the energy could be recovered, and A11 found 23 % of it available. The 86 J ESR bar has its own history: no script modelled the bank's series resistance until 2026-07-30, so the loss was real in hardware and absent from the accounting until a circuit simulation found it.

GMAT cross-check, the first external result, and it found something

GMAT R2022a, run headless with MSISE90 / 20x20 gravity / RK89, propagated to the 120 km floor at three solar-activity levels. The x1.80 lifetime multiplier of the day reproduces at mean and high activity. At low activity it does not, and the invariance the paper nominated as its defensible result is falsified. These runs were propagated at 20.37 m/s, before the measured sled mass moved the rated point to 16.029 m/s, the falsification survives, since a uniform density scale cannot move a ratio at any Δv, but the absolute numbers are historical (P19).

Spread 18.48 % against a ≤5 % band declared before the run, A5's verdict is FAIL. The mechanism was tested, not guessed: astro.py models solar activity as a uniform multiplicative scale on density, and sweeping that scale over a 40x range moves the multiplier only 1.7992 to 1.7968. A uniform density factor divides both lifetimes by the same number, so the ratio is invariant by construction of the model. MSIS instead changes the shape of the density, altitude profile with F10.7, the boosted orbit's apogee sits ~37 km higher, and the ratio then moves. Written up as P16.

Separately, over a bounded 30-day window the fitted decay rates are −0.1618 km/day (GMAT) against −0.1216 km/day (astro.py), GMAT decays 1.33x faster. Reported SMA is osculating and its short-period variation runs 12.2 km peak to peak, several times the decay across the window, so the comparison is a least-squares rate over 31 daily samples rather than a difference of endpoints.

What has actually been verified

Nineteen of twenty-one specified cross-checks have run, each against an acceptance band declared in writing before the script existed. Two failed outright, several missed individual bands, one found a published number 37 % high, and one returned three rows that could not be evaluated at all. That mix is the point of declaring the band first. Nothing has been validated by hardware — every row below is one model against another model.

The failures are the reason to trust the rest. The paper once carried four numbers its own scripts did not reproduce, all found by rebuilding the analysis from scratch. An independent propagator then falsified a claim in the paper's own abstract, and that is recorded as P16 rather than quietly dropped. Twice this month a band failed and the investigation found the bug in my own analysis script rather than in the design — which is exactly what a band declared beforehand is for. Every defect is numbered and published, including the ones that damage the project's own claims.
